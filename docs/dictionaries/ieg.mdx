# Sircar, *Indian Epigraphical Glossary* (`IEG`)

`IEG` is D. C. Sircar's *Indian Epigraphical Glossary* (1966) — a specialist glossary of the
**technical terms (administrative, fiscal, legal, social) that occur in Indian inscriptions**,
each defined and tied to the epigraphical corpus it comes from. Glosses are in **English**.

It is one of the most *independent* datasets on CDSL: measured against the rest of the
corpus, **57.5 % of its headwords appear in no other digitized dictionary here** (second
highest of all 44), which is exactly what you would expect of a work that indexes a document
genre — stone and copper-plate records — that literary lexicons barely touch.

## When to use it

Reach for `IEG` when reading **inscriptions** — or modern editions of them — and you meet an
administrative, fiscal, legal, or institutional term that a literary dictionary will not gloss
in its technical sense. Typical wins:

1. **Revenue and land terms** — classes of land tenure, tax names, measures.
2. **Administrative titles** — offices of the court, provincial and local administration,
   military ranks.
3. **Religious-institutional vocabulary** — temple offices, endowment formulas, monastic
   terms as inscriptions actually use them.

For general meanings use **[Monier-Williams (MW)](mw)**; for *names* (persons and places)
from the same inscriptional world use **[PGN](pgn)**.

## Reading an entry

Each entry is a **term** (`<k1>`) with the inscription corpus it occurs in and an English gloss.
The first entry (`csl-orig/v02/ieg/ieg.txt`):

```
<L>1<pc>001<k1>AbADa<k2>AbADa
{%ābādha%}¦ (CII 1), illness.
```

| In the source | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `{%ābādha%}` | the term (search key `<k1>` is SLP1) |
| `(CII 1)` | the source — *Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum*, vol. 1 |
| `illness` | the English gloss |

Many entries have **numbered senses**, each with its own citation trail, because the same term
behaves differently in different record types. Real example (`csl-orig/v02/ieg/ieg.txt`, entry
57), lightly wrapped for readability:

```
<L>57<pc>004<k1>AcArya<k2>AcArya
{%Acarya%}¦ (<ls>EI</ls> 33; <ls>BL</ls>), title of a religious teacher;
used commonly by the Buddhists and Jains (<ls>LL</ls>; <ls>SII</ls> 1).
<P>¦ (<ls>CII</ls> 3; <ls>EI</ls> 32; <ls>ML</ls>), a spiritual guide or teacher; a preceptor.
<P>¦ Same as {%Sthan-acarya%} (<ls>EI</ls> 17), a temple priest.
<P>¦ (<ls>EI</ls> 8; <ls>IA</ls> 14), an architect; used in the sense of 'a master',
i. e. 'a master-mason'; cf. {%Acari%} …
```

What to notice:

| Convention | Marker / form | Example | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Search key | SLP1 (technical term) | `AbADa` | k1 = k2 |
| Body form | IAST italic `{%…%}` | `{%ābādha%}` | The printed form |
| Corpus source | `(CII N)` etc. | `(CII 1)` = *Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum*, vol. 1 | Where the term appears in the epigraphic record |
| Full citation | `<ls>` + volume/page | `<ls>Ep. Ind.</ls>, Vol. IX, p. 151` | Longer form used inside prose notes |
| New sense | `<P>¦` at line start | sense 2, 3, 4… | Each sense carries its own sources |
| Cross-reference | `cf.` / `See` | `see {%abhaya-mudra%}` | Points to the head entry for compounds |

The glosses are terse but precise about *register*: under `{%abhaya%}`, sense 1 is the
hand-pose (`{%mudra%}`) on images, cited from *Epigraphia Indica* vol. 24, while sense 2 is
"place of security to persons in distress" — with a Tamil equivalent noted — keyed to siglum
`SITI` in Sircar's own source list. That bilingual aside is typical: South-Indian
inscriptional practice often surfaces Tamil forms.

## Where the citations point

Sircar keys almost every sense to a named inscription series. The sigla you will meet most are
the multi-volume national corpora and epigraphy journals — `CII` (*Corpus Inscriptionum
Indicarum*), `EI` (*Epigraphia Indica*), `IA` (*Indian Antiquary*), `SII` (*South-Indian
Inscriptions*) — alongside journal sigla such as `IHQ`. `IEG`'s printed key to these
abbreviations survives as **scanned front matter** (linked from
[csl-doc](https://github.com/sanskrit-lexicon/csl-doc/blob/master/source/dictionaries/prefaces/iegpref.rst));
its legend is not yet transcribed machine-readably on this site — see
[Abbreviations compared](abbreviations-and-citations) for where `IEG` stands. With roughly
**11,400 `<ls>` citation tags** across the file, a sense without a source pointer is the
exception, not the rule.

## Search tips

1. **Think compounds.** The headwords are technical compounds (`{%abhaya-mudra%}`,
   `{%daṇḍādhipa%}`); if the compound misses, try the members.
2. **Try both spellings.** Sanskritized Prakrit and vernacular forms sit beside classical
   ones (`{%acara%}` next to `{%ācāra%}`).
3. **Read every sense.** An inscription term frequently has a mundane meaning in one corpus
   and an office-title meaning in another; the `<P>` paragraphs keep them separate.
4. **Follow the `cf.` links.** Synonym chains (`{%Daṇḍādhipa%}` → `{%Daṇḍapāla%}`,
   `{%Daṇḍādhipati%}`) map the terminology of one administrative sphere.

## What makes it distinctive

- **Built for inscriptions.** Administrative/technical senses, keyed to the epigraphical corpus.
- **The standard reference** for epigraphical terminology (Sircar was the leading epigraphist
  of his generation and the general editor of *Epigraphia Indica*).
- **Highly independent headword stock** — the majority of its terms occur nowhere else on
  CDSL (57.5 % unique share per the site's atlas measurements, see
  [Guides Hypotheses](../about/guides-hypotheses)).

## Headword key conventions

`<k1>` = `<k2>` = SLP1 (epigraphical technical term). Glosses are terse English definitions with a corpus source in parentheses.
