# How to read Böhtlingk–Roth (PWG)

Böhtlingk & Roth's *Sanskrit-Wörterbuch* — the **Großes Petersburger Wörterbuch**, seven
volumes, 1855–1875 — is the deepest citation apparatus in Sanskrit lexicography, and the
first page of any entry throws its abbreviations at you: `R.`, `H.`, `P. 6,1,116`,
`Verz. d. B. H.` This page is the **dictionary-literacy pack**: what the editors say the
book is, which sigla you will actually meet (ranked by how often the digital text really
uses them), and how to tell a literary citation from a grammarian's rule or a manuscript
source.

For the **online lookup mechanics** (displays, input schemes, entry markup) see the
[PWG dictionary guide](../dictionaries/pwg); for the full transcribed front matter with
English and Russian translations see [OCR'd prefaces](../dictionaries/ocr-prefaces).

## What the Vorwort declares

The Foreword to volume 1 (1855) states the design in its own words
([English translation](https://github.com/sanskrit-lexicon/PWG/blob/main/prefaces/pwgpref02.en.md)):

> Our dictionary consists of two elements not entirely resolvable into one another: the
> material of the word-collections made by learned Indians, and our own collections drawn
> from Sanskrit literature.

That sentence explains most of what you will see on the page. Indian lexica (Amarakośa,
Hemacandra, the Medinī…) are reproduced **and** checked against real usage, so entries
record where the two disagree — the editors insist on keeping even the "residue of Indian
lexicography that does not resolve into actual usage" rather than discarding it. The other
declared commitment is **Veda first**: the editors treat complete excerption of Vedic
literature as "the right beginning for our work"
([Foreword, 2](https://github.com/sanskrit-lexicon/PWG/blob/main/prefaces/pwgpref03.en.md)),
and read the hymns from the texts themselves — Sāyaṇa and the commentators are demoted to
"only one of the aids", not the standard of interpretation
([Foreword, 3](https://github.com/sanskrit-lexicon/PWG/blob/main/prefaces/pwgpref04.en.md)).

The Foreword also declares the **search conventions** a reader needs
([Foreword, 5](https://github.com/sanskrit-lexicon/PWG/blob/main/prefaces/pwgpref06.en.md)):

- Verbal roots are listed **without** the vowels *ṛ, ṝ, ḷ* and without final diphthongs;
  final *-ṛ* of nominal stems is written *-ar*.
- Denominatives stand under their **suffix**, not in root form.
- The accent is marked in a simplified notation (Vedic examples keep the manuscripts'
  marking), and **no verb class is given** — it follows from the quoted 3rd sg. present.
- The work was divided: **Roth** covered Vedic literature, Suśruta's Āyurveda and plant
  names; **Böhtlingk** the rest and the arrangement of the whole.

## The sigla you will actually meet

The front matter legend declares **395 abbreviations**; the digital body actually uses 257
of them (65%). Frequency is heavily concentrated — the twenty sigla below account for the
bulk of all citations, and the very shortest marks (single capital letters) were assigned
to the works cited most. Counts are measured occurrences in the digital text
(`csl-orig/v02/pwg/pwg.txt`), from the site's legend feed
[`pref-legends.json`](https://github.com/sanskrit-lexicon/csl-guides/blob/main/src/data/pref-legends.json):

Two habits worth forming immediately:

- **A siglum's numbers point into one specific edition.** The legend entry tells you which:
  `MBh.` means the Calcutta edition; `R.` without further mark means Schlegel's edition
  for books 1–2 and Gorresio's for books 3–6; `Suśr.` is cited by volume, page and line of
  the Calcutta print. Reading a citation without knowing its edition is how misquotations start.
- **The single letters are earned, not arbitrary** — `R.`, `H.`, `N.`, `M.`, `P.` mark the
  five workhorses of the whole dictionary. Learn these five and a third of the apparatus
  reads itself.

The full legend, with every declared abbreviation, its expansion and its measured count, is
rendered on the [PWG guide's abbreviations section](../dictionaries/pwg#abbreviations).

## Grammarians' rules are not text citations

The legend feed classes the 395 keys as **393 work sigla, 1 grammar siglum, 1 meta
siglum** — but the learner-relevant split is functional. A cluster of sigla cite
**grammatical authority**, and their references read differently:

| Siglum | Authority | Body citations | How to read the reference |
|---|---|--:|---|
| `P.` | Pāṇini's *Aṣṭādhyāyī* | 95,965 | `P. 6,1,116` = book, chapter, sūtra — a **rule** |
| `Kâty.` | Kātyāyana (vārttikas on Pāṇini) | 8,789 | usually via the Pāṇini apparatus |
| `gaṇa.` | the gaṇapāṭha in Böhtlingk's Pāṇini ed. | 8,419 | membership in a listed word-class |
| `Siddh. K.` | *Siddhāntakaumudī* | 1,407 | the later grammatical compendium |
| `Pat.` | Patañjali's *Mahābhāṣya* (via Böhtlingk's excerpts) | 1,152 | the great commentary on Pāṇini |
| `Sch.` / `Schol.` | "Scholiast" — the commentator on whatever text is at hand | — | a **meta** marker, not one fixed work |

When an entry cites `P. 6,1,116.` it is telling you *this form is licensed by that rule* —
a statement about grammar, not an attestation in literature. The distinction matters
because the Vorwort's critical stance (usage beats the glossators) applies to word
*meanings*; for word *formation* the grammarians remain the cited authority.

## The rare and manuscript sigla are intentional

395 declared abbreviations against 257 used is not sloppiness — the gap is **typed and
explainable**, and the legend was never meant to be a checklist of the body:

- **The legend grew with the work.** Each later volume opens with its own front matter,
  including "abbreviations newly added" lists (volume 3's list is where the Buddhist
  manuscript sources first appear —
  [Foreword, 3-2](https://github.com/sanskrit-lexicon/PWG/blob/main/prefaces/pwgpref19.en.md)).
  Seven of the 27 transcribed front-matter pages are abbreviation legend.
- **Manuscript sources are first-class citizens.** From volume 3, A. Schiefner supplied
  readings from Buddhist manuscripts in the Paris library: `SADDH. P.`
  (*Saddharmapuṇḍarīka*, 171 body citations), `UPAG. AV.` (*Upaguptāvadāna*, 15),
  `DAŚABH.` (*Daśabhūmīśvara*, 12), `KAṬHINĀV.` (3), down to `KĀPIŚĀV.` and
  `KRIYĀSAM.` with a single citation each. A siglum with one hit is a deliberate record of
  a source consulted, not an OCR artifact. `Kâty. Śr.` — cited **8,694** times — is
  likewise marked `Hdschr.` (manuscript) in the legend, and Weber's *catalogue* of the
  Berlin manuscripts (`Verz. d. B. H.`) alone accounts for 4,677 citations.
- **The measured residual is typed.** The census leaves 133 legend keys with no body match
  (pref-only); the feed carries a typed residual class on 52 keys: 22 `true_unused` (mostly
  long bibliographic titles of European scholarship), 12 `rare` (manuscript and
  occasional works), 9 `ocr_key` (transliteration specials like `ḱ`, `Ǵ`), 5 `spacing`
  (dual keys such as `Vop. oder Vopad.` — the body always uses the short form, so the
  double key itself scores zero), 3 `ambiguous`, 1 `ortho` (German *j* for *y*). The full
  decomposition: [pref-only rollup](https://github.com/sanskrit-lexicon/csl-guides/blob/main/scripts/out/PWG_PW_pref_only_decompose.md).

So when you meet an unfamiliar siglum, the order of hypotheses is: a later volume's
addition → a manuscript source → a European reference work — and only then a transcription
problem.
